The story

In 1998 my aunt Lulu inherited a farm in western Uganda that had grown nothing for thirty years. She turned it into the first Fairtrade vanilla operation in Africa — around 2,000 farming families — and the profits built a school on the estate. That’s the story, and if you only say one line about us, say that one.

This isn’t a white-label jar with a logo on it. It’s one farm, one family, and a crop that has to be pollinated by hand — every single flower. The longer version, if you want it:

1920s
My great-grandfather, Major Trevor Price, drives from Cairo into western Uganda in a Model T Ford to grow tea. He buys Ndali in the Kingdom of Toro in the sixties. The soil turns out to be wrong for tea, and the land is left to grow wild.
1970s
Amin expels Europeans from Uganda and the estate is confiscated. It sits abandoned for the best part of thirty years while the rainforest takes it back.
1991
Uganda invites dispossessed landowners to return. His son Mark comes back and starts rebuilding.
1998
Mark dies suddenly. His niece — my aunt Lulu Sturdy, at the time a furniture maker in Oxfordshire — takes on the farm. She tries pyrethrum, chillis, rice and coffee before landing on vanilla. Mark’s son, my cousin Aubrey Price, takes on the lodge. The family splits the estate between them and both are still there: Aubrey runs Ndali Lodge on the crater rim, Lulu runs the vanilla.
2005
The co-operative Lulu buys from becomes the first Fairtrade-certified vanilla organisation in Africa — around 2,000 farming families around the Rwenzori foothills. The premium since then has paid for a pharmacy, a maize mill, school roofing, beekeeping and medical grants.
2017
Lulu co-founds Sadhguru School on the estate — 93 children aged three to ten, the poorest rural families in the district, on a Montessori and Cambridge curriculum. It’s the only school of its kind in East Africa. The vanilla is what pays for it.
Now
I’m Joe. Sturdy Vanilla is me bringing my aunt’s vanilla to the UK — that’s the whole business.

What a video has to do

Three steps. That’s the whole brief.

1

Open it on camera

Break the seal, tip the pods out, split one lengthways, smell it. The unboxing moment is the only reason anyone watches past two seconds.

2

Actually cook with it

Bake something, make custard or ice cream, stir it into coffee. A real recipe with a real result. Scrape the seeds out on camera — that shot alone sells the product.

3

Your face, your voice, an honest verdict

Taste it and say what you think. If it’s not for you, say so — that’s more useful to us than a five-star read-out nobody believes.

What doesn’t work: holding an unopened pack up to the lens saying it’s amazing. We’ve had those. They sold nothing, and they cost you the same effort as a good one.

Openers that have legs

This vanilla has to be pollinated by hand. Every single flower. Within eight hours. Leads with the most surprising true fact in the category.
Supermarket vanilla pods weigh two grams. This one’s seven and a half. Put both on camera. The visual does the arguing for you.
One tin of this powder is six vanilla pods. Nobody is using this and it’s the strongest value line we have.
The farm this comes from pays for a school. Ninety-three kids. The impact line. Concrete number, no charity-appeal tone.

If you want a shortcut to the idea

Borrow the structure. Bring your own kitchen.

Entirely optional — the three steps above are the only thing we actually need. But if you’d rather not start from a blank page, this is the method we use ourselves:

1

Search your own niche, not ours

Baking, coffee, custard, whatever you already make. Your audience came for your thing — vanilla is the ingredient, not the topic.

2

Find an outlier — 5× the views of the creator’s follower count

That multiple is the whole filter. It means the video travelled well beyond its own audience, so the idea did the work rather than the following. A big account’s big numbers tell you nothing.

3

Take the hook and the topic. Leave everything else.

Borrow how it opens and what it’s about — never the content itself. The recipe, the voice and the verdict have to be yours, or your audience clocks it instantly and the algorithm has already seen it.

The lines that matter

Please do
  • Call it “Ndali” — that’s the vanilla. Sturdy Vanilla is the shop.
  • Say it’s grown by my aunt in Uganda. That’s the honest framing and it’s the whole appeal.
  • Use the real numbers — 1998, 2,000 farmers, 93 children, 8 hours, 7.5g.
  • Tag the product so the sale tracks back to you.
Please don’t
  • Say “Ndali Vanilla” in the voiceover — it stutters against the brand name. Captions are fine.
  • Claim we own the estate. It’s family-shared and we’re the UK seller.
  • Mix up Ndali Lodge (my cousin Aubrey’s) with Ndali Vanilla (my aunt Lulu’s). Same estate, two businesses.
  • Make health, medical or weight-loss claims. It’s a food ingredient.
  • Say the school was founded by us — my aunt co-founded it.
